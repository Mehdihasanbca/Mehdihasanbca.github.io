Candidate guide / CV + evidence

A Gulf-job CV should prove what you can do—not try to look expensive.

A recruiter or employer usually needs to understand your role in seconds. Clear job titles, consistent dates, real duties and useful work evidence matter more than decorative templates or exaggerated claims.

Prepared by: Assignment Venue CenterReviewed by: Mehdi Hasan, Founder & Business HeadUpdated: 20 August 2026

On this page

1. What a screening CV must answer2. Recommended structure3. Write experience accurately4. Skills that mean something5. Work video6. Introduction video7. Quality control8. What not to do
AVC does not recommend fabricated experience, false certificates or altered documents. Profile support should improve clarity, not manufacture eligibility.

A CV for an overseas recruitment process has a practical job: help a screener decide whether your real experience matches a specific requirement. Many candidates approach the CV as a design exercise—more colours, bigger headings, more claims. That often makes screening slower rather than better.

The strongest profile is easy to verify. Dates make sense. Duties match the job title. The candidate can explain the same work in an interview. Certificates, licences or videos support the same story instead of creating a second story.

1. A screening CV should answer six questions quickly

  1. What job are you applying for?
  2. How many years of relevant experience do you actually have?
  3. Where did you gain that experience?
  4. What duties, tools, machines, vehicles or systems have you used?
  5. What is your most recent role?
  6. How can the employer contact and identify you for the process?

If these answers are buried below a career objective, long personal statement and unrelated courses, restructure the page.

2. A simple two-page structure is usually enough

SectionWhat to include
HeaderName, target role, phone, email, current location
Profile summary2–4 lines describing genuine role, years and strongest relevant experience
Core skillsSpecific tasks/tools relevant to the vacancy
Work experienceEmployer/project, location, job title, dates, duties
Education/tradeRelevant education, ITI/trade training or licence where applicable
AdditionalLanguages, driving licence categories, availability or role-specific information

Passport information should be included only if the process requires it. Avoid publishing full passport or identity numbers in a CV that may circulate widely unless there is a clear recruitment need.

3. Write experience so another person can test it

“Worked as electrician” is too broad. “Installed cable trays and conduits, pulled LV cables, terminated DB panels, used multimeter for basic fault checks, followed permit and PPE procedures” gives a screener real material to assess.

The same principle applies across trades:

  • Driver: vehicle types, route type, licence class, city/highway experience, basic checks, loading/passenger responsibility.
  • Welder: process used, material, position, thickness range where genuinely known, drawing/WPS familiarity, project environment.
  • Cook: cuisine, section, volume, prep responsibilities, equipment, hygiene practices.
  • Sales: product category, customer type, POS/billing, targets, stock handling, language ability.
  • Mechanic: vehicle/equipment types, diagnostics, repair scope, tools and systems handled.
Use verbs that describe real workInstalled, inspected, repaired, prepared, operated, diagnosed, assisted, supervised, sold, stocked and maintained are useful only when you can explain what followed the verb.

Dates should be internally consistent. If two jobs overlap, make clear whether one was part-time, project-based or simultaneous. Do not increase years by rounding aggressively. “June 2022 to August 2026” is more credible than turning four years and two months into “6 years.”

4. Replace vague skills with role evidence

Lists such as “hardworking, honest, good communication, team player” are not useless, but they do not differentiate a technical profile. Give priority to skills the employer can evaluate.

For example, instead of “computer knowledge,” a storekeeper could list inventory entry, barcode handling, Excel stock sheets and goods receiving. Instead of “good driving,” a light driver could list manual/automatic vehicles, route navigation, defensive driving and pre-trip checks—if true.

Do not list equipment you have only seen once. An interview question such as “Which parameter do you check first?” can expose copied skill lists quickly.

5. A work video should show the work, not the candidate posing beside it

Some Gulf vacancies ask for working videos because the employer cannot conduct an immediate trade test. The video should help them see your control, sequence, safety and familiarity with the task.

Useful work-video structure

  1. Start with a short identification: name and role only if requested.
  2. Show the full working area so the task is understandable.
  3. Demonstrate one complete, relevant task rather than many disconnected clips.
  4. Keep hands, tools and result visible.
  5. Use normal speed unless a close-up is needed.
  6. Do not perform unsafe work for the camera.
  7. Do not film confidential employer areas or people without permission.

A 60–120 second video of real work is often more useful than a five-minute montage. If another person is doing the task while you stand in frame, the video does not prove your skill.

6. Introduction videos: short, audible and consistent

For roles that require Arabic or English communication, the employer may ask for an introduction video. The goal is normally to assess basic communication, confidence and role relevance—not cinematic quality.

A practical structure is: name, nationality/current location if requested, role, years of relevant experience, one or two key duties, and availability. Use language you can actually speak. Memorising an advanced Arabic paragraph that you cannot understand creates the wrong expectation for a customer-facing job.

7. Run a consistency check before sending the profile

Compare CV, passport, certificates, application form and video. Names and dates should not conflict. If your passport uses a different spelling from an old experience certificate, flag the discrepancy rather than silently editing an official document.

Five-minute quality control

  • Phone and email are current.
  • Target role matches the vacancy.
  • Dates are chronological and believable.
  • Duties are specific and can be explained.
  • No invented employer, project, qualification or licence.
  • PDF opens correctly on a phone.
  • File name identifies you and the role.
  • Video audio and image are usable before upload.

8. What not to do

Do not buy a fake experience letter

A document can look professional and still create a false employment record. If checked with the company, during a trade test or through interview questions, it can damage the entire application. More importantly, false documents can have legal consequences.

Do not copy another person's project history

Project names are easy to copy and hard to explain. Your own smaller but real experience is more defensible than a famous refinery or airport project you never worked on.

Do not alter a certificate to match a new role

Keep official records intact. If your actual duties developed beyond the old job title, explain the duties in the CV and interview rather than changing the certificate.

Do not hide a lack of experience where fresher hiring exists

Some employers accept freshers or helpers and train them. A truthful entry-level profile can be selected for the right vacancy; a false senior profile can fail later.
